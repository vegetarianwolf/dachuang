The role of governmental venture capital in value creation for investee

firms: Evidence from Chinese government guidance funds

Xinfei Huang
International School of Business & Finance and Institute of Advanced Finance, Sun Yat-sen
University, China

International School of Business & Finance and Institute of Advanced Finance, Sun Yat-sen

Abstract
We study the role of Chinese government guidance funds (GGFs) in value creation for investee
firms. Using a sample of 2,855 firms that went public during the period of 2010-2021, we show
that GGF-backed IPO firms had higher initial returns than non-VC-backed IPO firms and non-
GGF VC-backed IPO firms. After decomposing the initial return into IPO underpricing and
market overvaluation, we find that GGF-backed firms enjoyed higher overvaluation and lower
underpricing than other firms. Consistent with investor sentiment and information asymmetry
hypothesis, our results indicate that public investors value the benefits of political resources
more than the costs of  government  interference associated with GGF sponsoring. However,
GGF-backed firms did not outperform other-VC-backed firms when post-IPO long-term stock,
operating  and  innovation  performance  is  assessed.  The  divergence  in  the  effects  of  GGFs
observed in the financial and product markets reveals the complexity in evaluating the role of
GGFs in value creation.

Keywords:  venture  capital,  government  guidance  funds,  IPO  initial  return,  underpricing,
market overvaluation, post-IPO performance

1. Introduction

Over the past decade, the venture capital (VC) market in China has evolved quickly despite

its short history since 2000, becoming the second largest in the world (only behind the US) and

increasingly attractive to global VCs.2  As of 2022, 19,354 VC funds have been registered with

the  Asset  Management  Association  of  China  (AMAC),  with  the  asset  under  management

reaching a total value of CNY 2.83 trillion (US$ 0.39 trillion).3  One important booster for the

growth of China’s VC market is the direct participation of the state as the capital provider of

VC funds. That is, the governments at various levels set up government guidance funds (GGFs)

to provide funding for firms that are considered to be important for economic development and

under-funded, analogous to the governmental venture capital (GVC) programs adopted in other

major markets.4  To understand the specifics of the GGFs is imperative for both the academia

and global VCs with an interest in entering the Chinese market, since the state plays a central

role in capital allocation and economic growth in China (Allen et al., 2022; Colinnelli et al.,

2022),  and  the  importance  of  GGFs  in  China’s VC  market  has  grown  rapidly  since  2015.5

While numerous studies have examined the effectiveness of GVC programs on stimulating the

growth  of  the  entrepreneurial  firms,  the  VC  market  and  the  local  economy  in  the  western

countries,  with  no  consensus  arrived  (e.g.  Lerner,  2000;  Avnimelech  and  Teubal,  2006;

Cumming, 2007; Alperovych et al., 2015; Brander et al., 2015; Cumming et al., 2017), scant

attention has  been paid  to  China’s VC market. Thus, to  fill this gap, this paper  investigates

whether and how GGFs create value for their investee firms in the financial market and the

product market.

GGFs have three distinguished features in comparison with other types of VC funds. First,

most of the GGFs rely on poorly incentivized governmental agencies or state-owned firms to

deliver  fund  management,  and  even  when  GGFs  cooperate  with  professional  private  fund

managers, it is common that the governments intervene the decision-making of fund managers,

which may reduce management efficiency (Lin, 2021). Second, GGFs have a dual mandate to

generate financial returns and to support the government’s policy goals (Luong et al., 2021).

The  distracted  profit  motives  of  GGFs  may  distort  the  capital  allocation  and  reduce  the

motivation of VCs to conduct costly coaching and monitoring activities to spur the growth of

the investee firms. Third, GGFs have natural ties with governments, which may bring valuable

political resources to support the growth of their investee firms. Given these characteristics of

GGFs, we ask the question of whether the benefits or the costs GGFs bring to the investee firms

dominate in practice.

We address the question in the context of initial public offerings (IPO) which is the most

important  exit  channel  for the VCs  in  the Chinese market (Chen, 2022). Using a sample of

2,855 firms that went public during the period between 2010 and 2021 in China, we firstly

show that the firms backed by GGFs (i.e. the firms receiving at least one investment from GGFs

prior to IPOs), have higher IPO initial returns (i.e. the stock return at the first day post IPO)

than the firms backed by non-GGF VCs (i.e. the firms receiving at least one investment from

VCs  without  funding  from  GGFs  prior  to  IPOs).  Importantly,  after  decomposing  the  initial

return  into  IPO  underpricing  (i.e.  low  offer  price  relative  to  firm  true  value)  and  market

overvaluation (i.e. high first-day closing price relative to firm true value), we find that the GGF-

backed firms enjoy lower underpricing and higher market overvaluation than the comparable

non-GGF VC-backed firms. The results are robust after addressing the endogeneity concern by

performing entropy balancing matching and Heckman two-step estimation, and are robust to

alternative methods of estimating firms’ true value.

The evidence, consistent with the information asymmetry theory, indicates that the public

investors perceive the funding from GGFs as a positive signal for firm quality, which makes it

less  necessary  for  GGF-backed  firms  to  depress  the  offer  price  to  attract  investors  in  the

presence  of  information  asymmetry  at  IPOs  (e.g.  Rock,  1986; Allen  and  Faulhaber,  1989;

Banerjee  et  al.,  2011;  Buchner  et  al.,  2019).  Drawing  on  the  investor  sentiment  theory,  the

evidence also indicates that public investors hold more optimistic attitudes towards the firms

backed by GGFs in comparison with the firms backed by other VCs. Overall, the results imply

that investors value the benefits associated with political connections more than the possible

costs associated with management inefficiency or capital misallocation brought by GGFs. This

creates value not only for the IPO firms by reducing the ‘money left on the table’, but also for

the investors by increasing the short-term financial returns they could earn from purchasing the

new shares.

Heterogeneity analysis shows that investors’ evaluation towards GGF-backed firms varies

across the stage and the amount GGFs invest in a firm. We conjecture that if a firm’s early VC

investments are made by GGFs rather than market-oriented VCs, the market may regard it as

an  uncompetitive  firm  that  lacks  attractiveness  to,  and  needs  support  from  GGFs  as  an

additional ‘selling point’ to attract attention from profit-seeking VCs. Also, we conjecture that

the amount of GGF investments relative to other VCs a firm receives represents the degree of

governmental intervention and management inefficiency, and the market  may  regard it as a

negative signal of firm quality. We find evidence in support of the conjectures by showing that

investors’ overvaluation towards the firms that receive GGF investments at an early stage, and

that  receive  more  GGF  investments  than  other-VC  investments  is  lower.  In  summary,  the

evidence  indicates  that  investors’  optimistic  sentiment  towards  GGF-backed  firms  can  be

diluted by the concern for the potential costs associated with the governmental intervention.

However,  GGF-backed  firms’  outperformance  at  IPOs  (i.e.  higher  initial  return)  do  not

persist post IPOs. We show that although the stocks of non-GGF VC-backed firms, on average,

outperform those of non-VC-backed firms by 0.5% per month over the two years post IPOs,

GGF-backed  firms  do  not  show  statistically  significant  differences  in  stock  performance  in

comparison with non-VC-backed firms. This indicates that the high valuation for GGF-backed

firms  at  IPOs  arises  from  a  temporary  overvaluation  by  the  market,  rather  than  from  real

benefits  GGFs  could  bring  up  to  effectively  improve  firms’  long-term  performance.

Consistently, we also find that the return on assets of the GGF-backed firms in the two years

following the  IPOs  is  on average lower than that  of the non-VC-backed  firms  by  0.6% per

annum,  whilst  there  is  no  statistically  significant  difference  in  the  operating  performance

between the non-GGF VC-backed firms and non-VC-backed firms. The results are robust to

alternative  measures  for  operating  performance.  Moreover,  we  find  no  evidence  that  GGFs

differ  from  other  VC  funds  in  stimulating  firms’  innovation  expenditures  or  innovation

productivity.  The  examination  of  firms’  performance  in  the  product  market  reinforces  the

implication  that  the  governmental  intervention  and  management  inefficiency  coming  along

with  the  GGF  capital  may  dominate  the  benefits  of  political  connections  when  it  comes  to

spurring the long-term growth of their investee firms.

Thus, this paper makes three important contributions to the literature. First, it adds to the

literature evaluating the role of governmental participation in the VC market. Extant literature

mainly  focuses  on  the  governmental  venture  capital  programs  carried  out  in  the  developed

markets such as the US (Lerner, 2000), Europe (Beuselinck and Manigart, 2000; Balboa et al.,

2007; Cumming and Johan, 2013; Grilli and Murtinu, 2014, 2015), Australia (Cumming, 2007;

Cumming and Johan, 2013; Cumming et al., 2017), and Israel (Avnimelech and Teubal, 2006),

and  looks  into  the  impact  of  these  GVC  programs  on  investee  firms’  product-market

performance and on fostering the development of VC market and economic growth. This paper,

to the best of our knowledge, is the first study evaluating the role of Chinese GGFs in value

creation for the investee firms, and thus contributes to the literature by adding insights into

China’s VC market, which represents the second largest market for innovative and high-growth

firms in the world (Colonnelli et al., 2022) and where the government plays a crucial role in

capital allocation and shaping the business environment across the country (Claessens et al.,

2008; Hsieh and Song, 2015; Jia et al., 2021). We show that the sponsoring by GGFs could

create value in the financial market both for the IPO firms by lowering the ‘money left on the

table’ and for the investors of purchasing the new shares by driving up the market overvaluation.

However, the value created in the financial market is shown to be driven by temporary investor

sentiment rather than the real benefits GGFs bring up to effectively improve the investee firms’

performance  in  the  product  market. The  ineffectiveness  of  GGFs  in  spurring  the  long-term

growth of investee firms adds supportive evidence to  the critiques of government  efforts  to

promote  VC  finance  (Lerner,  2010).  In  addition,  the  divergence  of  GGFs’  value  effects

observed in the financial and the product market contributes to the literature by revealing the

complexity of evaluating the value creation role of GGFs.

Second, the paper adds to the understanding of China’s VC market. In comparison with

capital and private equity market, concluding that the average fund manager disliked capital

providers with governmental ties. Their findings indicate that professional fund managers hold

a  negative  perception  on  governmental  participation  (i.e.  the  pros  of  political  connections

outweighing  the  cons  of  political  interference  in  decision-making)  in  VC  funding  in  the

Chinese market. However, our paper reveals an opposite pattern in the IPO market which is

dominated  by  unsophisticated  retail  investors;  that  is,  the  average  public  investor  holds

optimistic  attitudes  towards  the  IPO  firms  backed  by VCs  with  governments  as  the  capital

providers. Thus, our paper complements the literature on China’s VC market by identifying the

perception of stock market investors towards governmental participation in VC funding. Also,

the divergence between investors’ bullish attitudes towards GGFs and the underperformance

of GGFs in spurring long-term firm growth casts doubts on the ability of investors to evaluate

new shares in the Chinese market.

Third, the paper contributes to the understanding of the impact of political connections on

firms’  IPO  performance  in  the  Chinese  market.  There  is  evidence  that  the  firms  that  have

politically-connected top executives, or that hire underwriters with political connections have

a higher probability of obtaining IPO approvals (Chen et al., 2017; Wang and Wu, 2020). In

particular, Wang and Wu (2020) find that firms backed by politically-connected VCs (i.e. the

VCs  having  government  officials  as  top  executives)  have  higher  initial  returns  at  IPOs  and

worse post-IPO performance than firms backed by non-politically-connected VCs. They argue

that  the  firms  backed  by  politically-connected  VCs  are  less  mature,  of  lower  quality  and

depress the offer price at IPOs. However, while we also document higher initial returns of firms

disentangling underpricing from overvaluation when assessing IPO initial returns, in particular

in the Chinese market where the retail investors whose trading behaviors tend to be driven by

sentiment dominate.

2. Institutional background

2.1 China’s venture capital market and government guidance funds

China’s  venture  capital  market  has  been  quickly  evolving,  in  particular  over  the  past

decade, and became the second largest in the world (after the U.S.), despite its short history

since  2000.  Such  a  rapid  growth  can  be  largely  attributed  to  the  support  from  the  Chinese

government, either through indirect actions such as regulations or through direct actions such

as being the capital supplier (Chen, 2022).

On the one hand, a batch of regulations have boosted the development of the VC market

by  stimulating  the  demand  and  the  supply  of  the  venture  capital.  On  the  demand  side,  the

Chinese State Council launched the policy initiative “Mass Entrepreneurship and Innovation”

in  2015, potentially increasing the supply of high-tech and high-growth  entrepreneurship in

China  and  thus  the  demand  for  venture  capital.6  On  the  supply  side,  for  a  long  time,  large

financial institutions were not allowed to invest in China’s VC market, and such a restriction

started to loosen in recent years. For instance, insurance companies were allowed to invest in

venture  capital  funds  since  2014,  indicating  the  government’s  intention  to  introduce

sophisticated institutional investors and long-term capital providers to the VC market.7  The

tax policy in 2017 and 2018 allows VC investors to deduct 70% of the invested capital from

the taxable investment income to encourage funding for the venture capital.8  In addition, the

increase in the exit channels for venture capital investors also contributes to the growth of the

market.  In  2009,  2019  and  2021,  the  Shenzhen  ChiNext  exchange,  the  Shanghai  STAR

exchange, and the Beijing Stock Exchange was launched in succession. The establishment of

the new stock exchanges with lower listing criteria in comparison with the traditional ones (i.e.

the Shanghai Stock Exchange and the Main board of the Shenzhen Stock Exchange) enriches

entrepreneurial firms’ access to the public equity market (Bernstein et al., 2020) and thus may

attract funding from venture capital investors, in particular when the IPO serves as the most

important exit channel for Chinese VC funds accounting for 60% of the exits (Chen, 2022).

On the other hand, Chinese governments at various levels directly set up a special type of

VC funds, i.e. government guidance funds (GGFs) that are policy funds aimed at providing

capital  to  and  encouraging  non-government  investors  (known  as  the  ‘social  capital’  from

investment companies, wealthy families and individuals, and other VC/PE institutions etc.) to

invest in firms that are perceived as crucial for economic and social development and under-

funded.9  Most GGFs aim to raise 70 to 80 percent of the total funding from non-government

investors  (Zero2IPO,  2020).  Such  a  design  of  high  proportions  of  social  capital  in  a  GGF

intends  to  bring  profit  motives,  market  discipline  and  professional  expertise  into  industrial

policy  and  alleviate  the  pitfalls  of  inefficiency  and  corruption  associated  with  traditional

industrial policy tools (Lin, 2021).

2.2 China’s IPO market

China’s IPO market has been characterized by heavy government regulation and there are

some qualitative and ambiguous listing criteria for the candidate IPO firms. This may leave

room  for  discretions  in  the  IPO  approval  process.  For  instance,  IPO  candidate  firms  are

required  to  establish  good  governance  mechanisms  and  to  have  the  capacity  of  continuing

operation,  even  for  the  listings  at  the  Shenzhen  ChiNext  exchange,  the  Shanghai  STAR

exchange, and the Beijing Stock Exchange that have lower bright-line criteria in terms of net

profits and firm size, etc. than the traditional stock exchanges.13  Under this circumstance, the

regulator,  the  China  Securities  Regulatory  Commission  (CSRC),  and  more  specifically,  the

Stock Issuance Examination and Verification Committee consisting of seven members for each

application, of the CSRC plays an essential role in deciding which firms can finally go public

(Tian, 2011; Chen et al., 2017) and this makes political connections particularly valuable for

firms to obtain IPO application approvals (Liu et al., 2013). The GGFs, with governments at

A stylized fact is the prevailing high initial return (i.e. the stock return on the first day of

trading) of IPOs. The initial return of Chinese IPOs is extraordinarily high compared with other

main markets. Kimbro (2005), Song et al. (2014) and Qian et al. (2022) document an average

initial return on IPOs in China of 132% during 1995-2002, of 66.3% during 2006-2011 and of

172% during 1990-2021.14  While high IPO initial return is also prevalent in other markets, it

is believed that IPO pricing regulations and investor sentiment in particular contribute to the

extremely high IPO initial return in China’s market (Qian et al., 2022).

At  the  inception  of  China’s  IPO  market  in  the  early  1990s,  the  IPO  offer  price  was

determined in an administrative way, by a formula multiplying a P/E ratio of 13 to 15 set by

the CSRC and a company’s earnings over the past three years, while the pre-set P/E ratio was

much lower than the market-wise P/E ratio of traded stocks (Chueng et al., 2009). In 1999, the

book building approach, the dominant method of IPO pricing in most countries, was introduced

to China. However, this more market-oriented approach was suspended and replaced with the

administrative pricing approach since 2001 as the inability of the institutional and the retail

investors  to  deliver  reasonable  IPO  valuations  resulted  in  overheated  IPOs  with  very  high

offering P/E ratios in 2000. To limit the overvaluation of IPOs, the IPO pricing reform in 2006

modified the book building approach by setting the cap of the offering P/E at 30 (Song et al.,

2014) which was subsequently lifted in 2009.15  The release in the offering P/E ratio cap again

led to unreasonably high offering price, and this facilitated the issue of the regulation in 2012

that the offering P/E ratio should not, in general, exceed 125% of the average trading P/E ratio

for a given industry. While further modifications in the IPO pricing approach have been made

since  2012,  the  IPO  pricing  in  China  is  still  subject  to  regulatory  intervention  (known  as

‘Window Guidance’), with  the maximum offering  P/E  ratios far below what  investors were

willing to pay for most IPOs (Qian et al., 2022).

3. Literature review and hypothesis development

Venture  capital  funds  sponsored  by  governments  (typically  known  as  governmental

venture capital funds, GVC17), as a unique type of VCs, have been introduced to many countries

to foster the growth of entrepreneurial firms and local economy, e.g. the SBIR program in the

US, the IIF program in Australia, the LSVCCs in Canada, and the Yozma program in Israel.

Meanwhile, numerous studies evaluate the value effects of these programs and mixed results

are documented. On the one hand, some evidence suggests that the GVCs spur the sales growth

and employment of the investee firms (Lerner, 2000), boost the development of the venture

capital  industry  through  increasing  the  number  of  new  VC-backed  startups  and VC-backed

exits (Avnimelech and Teubal, 2006) and through increasing the total VC finance when GVCs

partner  with  private VCs  (Brander  et  al.,  2015),  and  providing  monitoring  and  value-added

An exception is Colonnelli et al. (2022) that surveys the perception of professional fund

China’s VC and PE markets. They show that the average GP dislikes investors with government

compared with the negatives of being intervened and controlled by the governments. However,

GPs are not the only market participant in the VC market that could be affected by governments.

The growth of the investee firms and the wealth of millions of public investors if the firms get

listed  on  stock  exchanges  may  also  be  affected  by  the  participation  of  governments  in  VC

investments. To shed some light on this issue, this paper examines the role of Chinese GGFs in

value creation for investee firms in the context of initial public offerings.

The pricing of shares at the initial offering determines the proceeds the issuing firms garner

from the capital raising, the short-term return the outside investors earn from purchasing the

new shares and also the wealth transferred from the shareholders of the issuing firms to public

investors  (Park  et  al.,  2015). While  the  issuing  firms  have  strong  intention  to  minimize the

‘money left on the table’ by minimizing the difference between the closing price on the first

day of trading and the offer price, substantial evidence has been found of high initial return

prevalent in most of the equity markets in the world (e.g. Beatty and Ritter, 1986; Kimbro,

2005; Song et al.,2014; Qian et al. 2022).

This  puzzle  of  high  IPO  initial  return  has  been  explained  mainly  in  the  theoretical

frameworks  of  information  asymmetry  and  investor  sentiment. The  information  asymmetry

hypothesis  states  that  IPO  firms  need  to  suppress  the  offer  price  to  induce  investors  to

participate in  the share purchases  since outside investors tend to  value firms  conservatively

13

Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=4496226

when they possess less information than the firms (Rock, 1986; Benveniste and Spindt, 1989;

Hanley, 1993;  Ritter and Welch, 2002;  Derrien, 2005; Banerjee et  al.,  2011;  Buchner et  al.,

2019).  Another  strand  of  literature  shows  that  in  an  environment  of  high  information

asymmetry, underpricing could be a signal of firm quality because only high-quality firms are

expected to recoup the loss at initial offerings after their performance is realized (e.g. Allen and

Faulhaber, 1989; Grinblatt and Hwang, 1989).

However, it is recognized that information asymmetry cannot fully explain the high initial

return and that optimistic investor sentiment could drive up the valuation of IPO firms (i.e. the

closing price at the first day of trading), contributing largely to the high initial return (Ritter

and Welch,  2002;  Sanders  and  Boivie,  2004;  Derrien,  2005;  Cook  et  al.,  2006;  Song  et  al.,

2014). Furthermore, Derrien (2005) relates the extent of underpricing (i.e. the pricing of IPO

shares below the true value in the primary market) to investor sentiment by demonstrating that

the bullish sentiment at the time of offering makes it less necessary for firms to underprice their

shares to attract investors.

The effect of investor sentiment may be in particular pronounced in China. This is because

China’s stock market is dominated by retail investors who are more likely to make sentiment-

driven trades than institutional investors (Chiang et al., 2010; Neupane et al., 2014), exerting

particularly strong effects on market overvaluation at IPOs (Derrien, 2005; Ljungqvist et al.,

2006; Dorn, 2009; Bajo and Raimando, 2017; Qian et al., 2022). Consistently, Song et al. (2014)

find that optimistic investor sentiment plays a dominant role over information asymmetry in

explaining the extremely high initial return in the Chinese market, by showing that the average

initial return of 66% for the IPOs of 948 Chinese firms can be attributed to an underpricing of

14-22% and an overvaluation of 44-53%.

Given the important role VCs play in the IPO market, there is an ongoing discussion on

how VCs affect the initial return. On the one hand, evidence is found that VCs can reduce initial

return  by  restraining  IPO  underpricing.  Under  the  information  asymmetry  hypothesis,  VC

sponsors have the ability to credibly communicate information about a firm’s true value, which

reduces the information asymmetry and thus IPO underpricing (Neus and Walz, 2005; Tian,

2011). In addition, the signaling hypothesis suggests that the outside investors would perceive

14

VC  sponsoring  as  a  certification  of  firm  quality  since  only  high-quality  firms  could  obtain

funding from VCs, and therefore less underpricing is needed to attract investors (Megginson

and Weiss, 1991; Park and Patel, 2015). On the other hand, Lerner (1994) and Ball et al. (2011)

argue  that  VCs  are  proficient  in  taking  their  portfolio  firms  public  near  market  peaks  and

therefore predict a positive effect of VC sponsoring on market valuation and consequently on

initial returns.

Would the injection of GGF funds alter the impact of VC sponsoring on IPO underpricing

and overvaluation? We propose that GGFs could make a difference in two different ways. On

the one hand, the political connections inherent in GGFs are scarce resources for the growth of

investee  firms  in  particular  in  the  Chinese  market.  Piotroski  and  Zhang  (2014)  argue  that

Chinese  politicians  tend  to  give  helping  hands  through,  for  instance,  access  to  capital,

government purchasing, and the authority to examine and approve projects (Tian et al., 2008),

to firms in their jurisdictions in order to stimulate local economy and consequently improve

their  political  credentials.  Consistently,  Wang  and  Wu  (2020)  find  that  firms  backed  by

politically-connected VCs (i.e. VC companies having top executives who serve as country head

or  higher)  have  a  higher  likelihood  of  obtaining  IPO  approvals  from  Chinese  Securities

Regulatory  Commission  (CSRC). Also,  Cao  et  al.  (2016)  document  a  positive  relationship

between political connections and the operating  performance of entrepreneurial firms in the

Chinese market. Thus, if investors recognize the valuable resources GGFs could bring to the

investee firms, they may hold more optimistic attitudes towards GGF-backed firms. This, in

line with the investor sentiment hypothesis, predicts a stronger valuation-amplifying effect of

GGF-backed  VC  sponsoring  than  non-GGF-backed  VC  sponsoring.  Consistently,  the  GGF

sponsoring could act as an additional layer of certification of firm quality in comparison with

general  VC  sponsoring  without  governmental  support.  This,  drawing  upon  the  signaling

hypothesis, predicts a stronger underpricing-reduction effect of GGF-backed VC sponsoring

than non-GGF-backed VC sponsoring. Following this line of thought, we hypothesize that (i)

GGF sponsoring has a positive effect on IPO firms’ market overvaluation, (ii) GGF sponsoring

has a negative effect on firms’ IPO underpricing, or (iii) both occur.

On the other hand, being sponsored by GGF-backed VCs can also be a signal of low firm

quality for two reasons.  First,  the mandate of GGFs to serve the government’s policy goals

suggests that GGFs’ distribution of capital may not be driven by profit motives and thus could

distort  the  capital  allocation  away  from  projects  with  the  greatest  growth  potential  in  the

economic  sense.  The  lack  of  economic  incentives  may  also  reduce  GGFs’  motivation  to

perform the coaching and monitoring activities that  are documented to be beneficial for the

investee firms (Groh et al., 2010; Dihiya and Ray, 2012; Bernstein et al., 2016; Cummings et

al.,  2017).  Second,  most  of  the  GGFs  are  managed  by  state-owned  firms  or  governmental

agencies (as discussed in Section 2) which are believed to be more likely to make inefficient

investment  decisions.  Even  if  GGFs  cooperate  with  professional  fund  managers,  anecdotal

evidence shows that there is deep governmental intervention in daily fund management. Thus,

such negative signals for firm quality may spur the conservative attitudes of investors towards

IPOs backed by GGFs, leading to lower IPO overvaluation. Consistently, the public investors

may  perceive  the  GGF  sponsoring  as  a  signal  of  increased  uncertainty  about  firm  quality,

making it more necessary for GGF-backed firms to depress the offer price in order to attract

investors  at  IPOs,  resulting  in  higher  IPO  underpricing.  Following  this  line  of  thought,  we

hypothesize that (i) GGF sponsoring has a negative effect on IPO firms’ market overvaluation,

(ii) GGF sponsoring has a positive effect on firms’ IPO underpricing, or (iii) both occur.

In  summary,  GGFs  could  exert  impact  on  IPO  overvaluation  (underpricing)  in  two

opposite ways, hence it is an empirical question of which force dominates in the practice. An

equally  important,  if  not  more,  question  is  whether  the  impact  of  GGFs  on  the  stock

performance at IPOs persists for a long period post IPOs. Moreover, besides exerting impacts

on firms’ performance in the financial market, do GGFs also affect firm operating performance

and innovation activities in the product market? These questions will be examined in details in

the empirical analysis section.

5.1 Variables and summary statistics

The dependent variable, the initial return of an IPO (Returnint) is calculated as the ratio of

the difference between the first-day closing price and the IPO offer price to the offer price,

5.2 Methodology

To mitigate the endogeneity issue associated with the possibility that covariates affect both

the probability of a firm being invested by VC funds and the initial return at IPOs, we perform

the  entropy  balancing  matching  (EBM)  following  recent  literature  (e.g.  McMullin  and

Schonberger,2020; Beck et al.,2022; Basu et al.,2022). The recently developed methodology

enables to eliminate the inequalities in representation with respect to the first, second or higher

moments  of  the  covariate  distributions  through  reweighting  the  sample  units  (Hainmueller,

2012; Hainmueller and Xu, 2013). In addition, it is recognized as a more effective matching

technique than conventional propensity-score matching and nearest-neighbor matching etc. in

terms of achieving covariate balance and retaining more information (e.g. Hainmueller, 2012;

Cazier et al., 2020). In the empirical analysis where EBM is applied, following the existing

literature,  we  match  the  treatment  group  and  the  control  group  based  on  all  observable

characteristics (i.e. the control variables as defined in Section 5.1).

8. Conclusion

Using a sample of 2,855 firms that went public during the period between 2010 and 2021

in China, we examine the role of GGFs in the value creation for investee firms in the financial

market  and  the  product  market.  We  show  that  GGF-backed  firms  enjoyed  higher  market

valuation and lower IPO underpricing in comparison with non-VC-backed firms and even non-

GGF VC-backed firms, indicating that GGFs could create values in the financial market both

for the issuing firms through minimizing the ‘money left on the table’ and for stock investors

through driving up the closing price on the first day of trading.

However, further evidence shows that the stock outperformance of GGF-backed firms at

IPOs (i.e. manifested by their higher initial returns) did not persist, disappearing within two

years  after  the  IPOs.  Consistently,  evidence  is  also  found  that  GGF-backed  firms

underperformed  the  non-GGF  VC-backed  firms  and  even  the  non-VC-backed  firms  in  the

product market post IPOs regardless of variables used to measure firm operating performance.

Moreover, we find no evidence that GGFs differ from other VCs in their effects on promoting

investee firms’ innovation expenditures or innovation productivity.

The results indicate that the higher initial returns of GGF-backed firms are more likely to

arise from temporary market overvaluation rather than from the real benefits GGFs bring to

their investee firms to improve their long-term performance. The evidence provides support to

our  conjecture  that  the  average  investor  in  China’s  stock  market  puts  more  weight  on  the

benefits of political resources than on the concerns of capital misallocation and management

inefficiency associated with GGF sponsoring when evaluating new shares.

The divergence of GGFs’ value effects observed in the financial and the product market

reveals the complexity of evaluating the value creation role of GGFs in Chinese market – while

GGFs do not create real values in the product market for investee firms, they can spur investors’

optimistic sentiment towards the firms which could create values (at least in the short term) in

the financial market. The evidence also casts doubt on the ability of the investors at large to

evaluate new shares, and highlights the crucial role of sentiment-driven trades in affecting stock

valuation  in  the  Chinese  market.  Following  this  line  of  thought,  it  justifies  the  efforts  the

Chinese regulators have made to promote the growth of institutional investors to stabilize the

market such as the qualified foreign institutional investors (QFII) regime, and various policy

initiatives to stimulate the growth the securities investment fund industry, etc.

In addition, given the increasingly important role of GGFs in China’s VC market, the paper

calls for attention from regulators to  taking actions to address the problems of management

inefficiency  and  possible  capital  misallocation  associated  with  GGFs  in  order  to  bring  real

benefits to stimulate the long-term growth and innovation activities of their investee firms.
