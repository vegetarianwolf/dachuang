## **1. Introduction** 

Entrepreneurial activity, represented by the creation of new companies, is one of the driving forces behind economic growth (Bertrand & Kramarz, 2002; Gourio et al., 2016; Reynolds, 1999). On the one hand, newly entrant enterprises bring about a “creative destruction” effect (Schumpeter, 1942) by introducing innovative technologies or novel forms of industrial organization, causing continuous disruption to the existing inefficient market equilibrium. As more entrepreneurs enter the market, economic rent decreases, and the market tends towards a more efficient equilibrium. On the other hand, the influx of new companies fosters competition, compelling incumbents to enhance their levels of innovation and productivity (Aghion et al., 2004, 2009). This, in turn, contributes to productivity improvements in an aggregated level (Foster et al., 2008). Given this perspective, the entry of new businesses within a region to some extent mirrors the local economic vitality. 

Facilitating the entry of new firms is typically one of the major objectives for many governments, especially at the subnational level. This endeavor is often pursued through methods such as reducing entry costs 

(Alesina et al., 2005), implementing tax cuts (Venancio et al., 2020ˆ ), providing subsidies (Accetturo, 2022), and more. Empirical findings also corroborate that the entry of newly established enterprises contributes positively to regional economic progression, exemplified by an increase in employment (Kirchhoff & Phillips, 1988; Fritsch & Mueller, 2004; Van Stel & Suddle, 2008), as well as by fostering advancements in overall productivity (Audretsch & Keilbach, 2004; Erken et al., 2018) or innovation (Modrego et al., 2015; Gumbau Albert, 2017). However, a substantial body of literature uncovers that in the presence of fiscal constraints, government behavior tends to be somewhat distorted. For example, in response to fiscal pressure, the government may expand informal charges (Liu, 2018), enhance tax enforcement (Chen, 2017), transfer expenditure obligations to companies (Wen et al., 2023), or intervene in the allocation of financial resources within its jurisdiction (Sun & Wang, 2020; Zhu et al., 2020). Consequently, do these behavioral changes of the government under fiscal pressure reduce the attractiveness for the entry of new enterprises, or even yield a repelling effect? What is the underlying mechanism at play here? The current body of literature fails to respond to these questions. Furthermore, the

China’s development experience may provide preliminary insights into the correlation between entrepreneurship and economic growth. From the late 1980s when the legitimacy of private sector was first acknowledged, to the end of 2021 when private enterprises constituted 92.1 % of all enterprises nationwide, China’s economy underwent a rapid expansion, ultimately propelling it to the position of the world’s second-largest economy. For an extended period, governments at various levels in China have maintained a proactive stance towards encouraging entrepreneurship. For instance, following Premier Li Keqiang’s introduction of the concept of “mass entrepreneurship and innovation” in the 2015 Government Work Report, the State Council has issued a series of policy directives aimed at guiding entrepreneurial endeavors throughout the entire society. At the subnational level, prefectural-level city governments have implemented numerous preferential policies tailored for newly registered business, such as offering low-interest loans, startup subsidies, rent subsidies for venues, and social security subsidies for entrepreneurs.[1] 

However, the fiscal condition of local governments in China has deteriorated sharply in recent years. Under the framework of fiscal decentralization,[2] , local governments in China have long grappled with the challenge of aligning tax revenues with diverse expenditure needs. Over the past decade, this challenge has been intensified by inclusive policies introduced by the central government and the effects of public health events. Notably, the implementation of the comprehensive “Business Tax to Value-Added Tax” reform in 2016, followed by a series of “tax cuts and fee reductions” policies in 2018, along with the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic at the end of 2019, have all contributed to a Guo & significant reduction in the fiscal revenue of local governments ( Shi, 2021; Lin et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022). In addition, the 2014 budget law imposed strict limits on the annual debt issuance quotas for local governments. That same year, the State Council’s issuance of “Document No. 43” further reinforced these financial constraints by severing the ties between local governments and their financing vehicles. These measures collectively imposed significant constraints on the avenues available to local governments for financing through debt. However, daily government operations and essential public expenditures exhibit a high degree of rigidity, often referred to as a "ratchet effect" (Hercowitz and Strawczynski, 2004; Lee & Plummer, 2007). Using economic indicator data for China’s prefecture-level cities spanning the years 2011 to 2021, our analysis reveals that local government fiscal pressure significantly hinders the entry of new enterprises. This finding maintains its robustness when incorporating alternative metrics for both explanatory and dependent variables, accommodating time lags in the explanatory and control variables, and redefining the geographical placement of firms. In the heterogeneity analysis, this inhibitory effect is consistently observed in the influx of non-state-owned enterprises (non-SOEs), while it is not evident in the context of state-owned enterprise (SOE) entries. Moreover, the primary inference stands firm in city samples characterized by intense financial resource manipulation or nascent inclusive financial development. During our investigation into the underlying mechanisms, we ruled out the potential of local fiscal pressures influencing new enterprise entries via government subsidies, tax enforcement or the local business climate. Instead, following a comprehensive examination of various databases related to local enterprises, our analysis suggests a “credit availability differentiation” pathway, wherein fiscal stress affects the scale of bank credit accessible to local businesses of varying sizes. 

To alleviate the potential endogeneity concerns in the main regressions, we introduce a causal identification framework based on a natural experiment. The implementation of the Fifty-Fifty Revenue Sharing Reform for Value-Added Tax (VAT) (hereafter VAT revenue sharing reform) in May 2016 significantly reduced the fiscal revenue of local governments in China, particularly impacting those cities where business tax constituted a significant proportion of fiscal revenue. This policy was part of a broader suite of tax reforms pursued by the Chinese central government, largely independent of the prevailing economic conditions and without directly affecting enterprise inflows or outflows. Therefore, it can be considered an exogenous shock that intensified fiscal pressures on local governments, allowing us to delineate control and treatment groups based on the varying intensities of policy impact. Subsequently, we employ a Difference-in-Differences (DID) model to validate the effect of fiscal pressure on enterprise entry. Furthermore, to explore the spillover effects of local fiscal pressures on enterprise entry in neighboring regions, we use spatial econometric models for analysis, taking into account the potential spatial interdependence between new business registrations and fiscal pressures across cities. After conducting relevant tests, the General nesting spatial model (GNSM) was selected. Besides, to ensure a more nuanced and accurate representation of these spatial relationships, our approach incorporates an array of diverse spatial weight matrices. The empirical results affirm the conclusion drawn from the main regression and further unveil an “expulsion effect” of fiscal pressure in neighboring cities on the establishment of new

## **2. Literature review and hypothesis development** 

## _2.1. Firm entry_ 

The concept of “new firm entry” discussed in this paper refers to the process by which newly established enterprises register and commence operations in a specific region. Alternatively, it can be interpreted as an increase in the number of new enterprises in a given area, reflecting the entrepreneurial vitality of that region. Studies concerning firm entry primarily centers on the factors that affect the initiation of a business in a specific place. This encompasses factors like personal traits of entrepreneurs, macroeconomic indicators, and government policies. Firstly, the initial founding of a business is greatly dependent on entrepreneur’s personal characteristics, where aspects like their gender (Klapper & Parker, 2011; Yang & Aldrich, 2014; Th´ebaud, 2015; Minniti, 2009), age (Evans & Leighton, 1989; Karoly & Zissimopoulos, 2004; Kautonen et al., 2014; Levesque & Minniti, 2006), educational level (Oosterbeek et al., 2010; Vanevenhoven & Liguori, 2013), and financial status (Buera, 2009; Gujrati et al., 2019) play pivotal roles. Secondly, alterations in the macroeconomic environment will also influence the entry of businesses into the region. For instance, empirical studies demonstrate that factors such as the growth of the digital economy (Zhao et al., 2022), diminishing trade policy uncertainty (Cui & Li, 2023), enhanced governmental efficiency (Du et al., 2020), and the adoption of industrial robots (Chen et al., 2023) are all conducive to the entry of new enterprises in a region. Conversely, escalating population aging (Feng & Li, 2023) and rising political uncertainty (Chen et al., 2020) have generally been observed to exert a negative effect. Thirdly, certain specific policies enacted by the government, though not explicitly aimed at the entry of new companies, will nonetheless exert an indirect impact. Da Rin et al. (2011) affirmed that variations in the tax rates imposed on companies are closely associated with corporate entry. Li (2020) posited that the reform allowing local governments in China to transfer land use rights through market transactions has increased the likelihood of enterprise entry by enhancing urban land use efficiency, promoting the development of urban infrastructure, and reducing urban rent-seeking corruption. Kong and Qin (2021) identified that China’s anti-corruption campaign has improved the entrepreneurial environment, primarily by curtailing rent-seeking opportunities and elevating the quality of government administration. Moreover, Li et al. (2022) discovered that China’s initial round of environmental inspections conducted by China’s central government notably decreased the entry of companies into highpolluting industries. Besides, Bai et al. (2022) confirmed the impact of the national innovative city pilot policy in fostering innovation, most 

prominently evidenced by the surge in entrepreneurial activity within the pilot cities. 

## _2.2. The impact of local fiscal pressure_ 

This study is also relevant to the literature on the impact of local fiscal pressure on regional macroeconomic environments or corporate behavior. When confronted with substantial fiscal deficits or a large scale of debt, the government’s foremost priority lies in devising strategies to augment revenue or cutting expenditures (Miller & Russek, 1997; Nelson & Balu, 2014). At the same time, it must contemplate methods to invigorate local economic development (Eisinger, 1988) and achieve political accomplishments that support official promotion (Que et al., 2019), all executed within the constraints of limited financial resources. Consequently, specific government actions motivated by selfinterest have the potential to impact the regional macroeconomic and ecological environment, as well as the behavior of micro-enterprises within their jurisdictions. In their study utilizing data from China, Han and Kung (2015) observed that when local governments experience a decline in fiscal revenue, they are more inclined to foster the growth of local real estate and infrastructure construction sector, as opposed to focusing on the industrial development. Slightly differing in their view, Xi et al. (2017) claimed that local governments, in response to financial pressure arising from a reduction in the VAT share ratio, will intensify their support for overcapacity industrial enterprises that can generate substantial VAT revenue. Ruan and Zhao (2022) uncovered that in response to fiscal pressure, governments in low-income regions tend to intensify tax collection and maintain a reliance on agricultural production. In contrast, governments in high-income regions persist in attracting enterprises through tax incentives and introduce substantial capital investments. These distinct tactics lead to regional disparities in economic growth. Furthermore, when constrained by limited fiscal options, such as tax incentives or subsidies, due to fiscal pressures, the government may resort to alternative resources, such as land resources and local financial resources, to attain its objectives. Qi et al. (2020) revealed that under financial pressure, China’s local governments tend to transfer land use rights at low prices as a strategy to attract investments. Zhu et al. (2020) contended that when facing fiscal pressures, local governments tend to interfere in the distribution of local credit resources and direct a larger share towards the state-owned economic sector. Beyond their economic effects, fiscal pressures also give rise to environmental challenges. Escalating fiscal pressures often cause a decrease in governmental attention to environmental matters, a rise in energy usage, and diminished advancements in green technology, subsequently heightening carbon emission levels (Xue et al., 2023). The research by Bai et al. (2019) indicates that regional tax competition, driven by local government fiscal pressures, negatively affects the local environment and reduces environmental quality in neighboring areas. ## _2.3. Hypothesis proposal_ 

The influence of government actions on micro-enterprise decisionmaking is a frequently debated subject within academic discourse. Such insights hold practical implications for the establishment of a “proactive government.” As demonstrated in Section 2.2, numerous scholars have engaged in discussions on this topic by examining changes in government behavior resulting from fiscal pressures. Regrettably, to the best of our knowledge, there exists a dearth of scholarly research exploring the influence of fiscal pressure faced by local governments on firm entry into the locality. However, existing empirical findings suggest that governments, when confronted with fiscal difficulties, may adopt measures such as reducing subsidies (Ilyas et al., 2022; Phomsoda et al., 2021), enhancing tax enforcement (Chen, 2017; Ruan & Zhao, 2022), and intervening in the allocation of financial resources (Sun & Wang, 2020; Zhu et al., 2020). These actions are likely to impede the creation of firms in the region. Considering this context, we present the primary hypothesis, H1, of our study. 

**H1** . Fiscal pressure on local governments impedes the entry of new enterprises. 

Research in the literature establishes that governments may manipulate the distribution of financial resources within their jurisdictions to serve their own objectives. Such actions may stem from the inherent responsibilities of governments, including efforts to stimulate economic growth (Andrianova et al., 2012; Gerschenkron, 1962) and rectify market failures (Besley, 1994; Spratt, 2009; Stiglitz, 1989). Alternatively, they might also be driven by self-serving motives, such as seeking to gain more votes (Cole, 2009; Dinç, 2005; Micco et al., 2007) or to facilitate the promotion of officials (Ji et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2019). In the specific context of China, although the central government possesses the access and supervisory rights over financial institutions, local governments still retain the capability to intervene in the credit allocation decisions of local financial institutions, either through holding stakes (Xu, 2018) or by employing administrative means (Yao & Peng, 2013). Zhou (2003) highlighted that local financial resources have evolved into what can be termed “secondary fiscal reserves” for local governments in China. Many scholars have also confirmed the phenomenon that local governments in China may mobilize local financial resources for purposes of political advancement or inter-regional competition (Qian et al., 2011; Sun & Wang, 2020; Tian et al., 2023). 

In addition, existing literature indicates that local governments in China are pro-business (Li & Zhou, 2005), particularly towards major and key enterprises within their jurisdictions (Nie et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2014). The main logic behind this comprises three key points. Firstly, these entities play a pivotal role in local economy by not only markedly propelling regional economic expansion and facilitating substantial investments but also serving as the principal contributors to local tax revenues. Secondly, large-scale enterprises often serve as the core of local industrial chains (Grzybowska, 2011; Yan, 2013), which are capable of attracting industrial clusters (Alfaro et al., 2019). Through mechanisms such as supply chain integration, knowledge spillovers, and the creation of labor pools, they exert a positive influence on the development of local small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) 

(Jannati, 2020; Lu et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2024; Owen-Smith & Powell, 2004; Ye & Zhuang, 2022). Thirdly, large local enterprises usually maintain close ties with local governments, sometimes even involving direct equity relationships. Consequently, they actively lobby for their own corporate interests and seek to influence the allocation of resources in their favor (Ji & Zhang, 2024; Luo & Tang, 2009). 

In light of this, under fiscal pressures, the inclination of local governments to favor large enterprises in resource allocation may be intensified. In scenarios where fiscal resources are scarce, local governments find it challenging to rely solely on public funds and fiscal tools to stimulate investment and promote local economic development (Di Liddo et al., 2019; Tang et al., 2023). At this juncture, local governments may offer greater conveniences to local large-scale enterprises, with the aim of enhancing their role in driving the local economy. Moreover, when confronted with fiscal constraints, local governments are further incentivized to engage in tax base protection (Chung & Williams, 2021; Ruan & Zhao, 2022). As a result, they are inclined to allocate additional resources to key revenue-generating enterprises to maintain their normal and sustained operations. Lastly, in times of fiscal distress, local governments may postpone or even default on payments owed to contractors engaged in major infrastructure projects, which are typically local large enterprises (Li et al., 2023). As a form of compensation, to some extent, the government may also channel a greater allocation of credit resources towards these enterprises through the exercise of administrative power. 

The preceding analysis raises an important question: under fiscal pressure, is it likely that local governments’ motivation to intervene in financial resources will increase, resulting in a larger allocation to local large enterprises? If this is the case, local SMEs’ credit resources will most likely be crowded out. SMEs in China have traditionally faced financial repression due to a lack of collateral assets and the absence of a well-developed multi-tier capital market (Li et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2022). When their potential credit space is further squeezed, the accessibility to financial resources becomes even more restricted. Given that financial resources play a crucial role in firm establishment and entrepreneurial activities (Di Patti & Dell’Ariccia, 2004), increased difficulty in accessing such resources, along with the resulting concerns over future credit availability, will directly contribute to a decline in the number of new local business registrations. Based on this reasoning, we put forth the second hypothesis (H2). 

**H2** . Local fiscal pressure leads to the crowding out of credit resources available to local SMEs. 

Expanding revenue sources and reducing expenditures are almost Nelson reflexive strategies for governments when facing fiscal pressures ( & Balu, 2014; Wolman, 1983). As a form of direct government expenditure, subsidies dispensed by the government are prone to diminution during intervals of fiscal adversity (Ilyas et al., 2022; Phomsoda et al., 2021). In fact, the reduction of subsidies can, indeed, sometimes result directly in significant expenditure savings for the government. For instance, in 2015, Indonesia’s reform of diesel and gasoline subsidies led to a notable saving of approximately 15.6 billion US dollars, marking a significant reduction in government expenditure for that year. Similarly, Egypt undertook a substantial cut in its fossil fuel subsidies in 2014, alleviating a considerable fiscal strain that had previously consumed more than 20 % of its 2013 budget. Nevertheless, given that government subsidies play a pivotal role in the sustainability of startup companies (Duhautois et al., 2015), the reduction of these subsidies for local enterprises, particularly SMEs, is likely to exert a “demonstration” effect on newly established businesses. Therefore, in the context of this study, should Chinese local governments opt to diminish various subsidies to companies as a response to fiscal pressures, such a course of action is anticipated to adversely impact the entry of new firms (Accetturo, 2022).

In times of fiscal distress, governments may intensify tax collection efforts as a strategy to generate additional revenue (Peng et al., 2023; Yu & Mai, 2022). Yet, entrepreneurial activities are notably sensitive to taxation, and an increased tax burden will markedly affect the establishment of businesses (Belitski et al., 2016; Da Rin et al., 2011). **H4** . Local fiscal pressure, through the enhancement of tax enforcement, negatively impacts the entry of new businesses. 

Empirical studies have provided evidence that an optimized business environment, encompassing factors such as improved governmental governance (Kaufmann et al., 2009), enhanced legal system construction (Sobel, 2008), a cheap and fast incorporation process (Klapper et al., 2011) and a decrease in corruption (Kong & Qin, 2021), can foster entrepreneurial activities. Correspondingly, scholars have also highlighted that an adverse business environment can act as an impediment to the entry of new enterprises (Hallward-Driemeier, 2009; Klapper & Richmond, 2011; Muzi et al., 2023). Furthermore, amidst financial constraints, the government might resort to actions detrimental to the local business climate. In addition to previously discussed measures such as intensifying tax collection efforts, reducing subsidies, and creating disparities in financial access for enterprises, there may also be initiatives involving intervention in market pricing (Jiang et al., 2022) and a reduction in the provision of government services (Cooper, 1996; Scorsone & Plerhoples, 2010). Therefore, does fiscal pressure affect the entry of new enterprises by harming the local business environment? To test this mechanism channel, we propose hypothesis 5. 

**H5** . Local fiscal pressure, by deteriorating the business environment, negatively impacts the entry of new businesses. 

## **3. Research design** 

## _3.1. Data and variables_ 

The data analyzed in this article encompasses both regional and corporate levels. At the regional level, the study incorporates data from 281 prefecture-level cities spanning the period from 2011 to 2021, yielding a total of 2630 valid observations. The economic and fiscal indicators for each city are obtained from their respective annual statistical yearbooks or bulletins and the China City Statistical Yearbook for each corresponding year. The patent application and registration data for prefecture-level cities are sourced from the website of the China National Intellectual Property Administration, while the business registration data for enterprises are derived from the National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System, which publishes corporate registration information.[3] At the corporate level, to include financial information from enterprises of various sizes, particularly SMEs in prefecture-level cities, we integrated two distinct databases: the National Taxation Survey Database and the financial data of listed companies from the CSMAR database. We then matched these data sets to their respective prefecture-level cities based on the location of each enterprise. In the robustness tests, we revised the criteria for determining the location of the enterprises to their actual places of operation. Constrained by the limitations in data availability, some databases do not cover the entire span of our study period. As a result, we endeavored to align the research intervals of various types of company samples with the primary regression analysis. Furthermore, we excluded company

Regarding the measurement of new business entry at the prefecturelevel city, we followed the approach of Kong and Qin (2021). This involved adding one to the number of newly registered enterprises per 10,000 people in each city annually, and then taking the natural logarithm of this figure to derive the business entry index ( _Entry1_ ). In calculating the number of business entries for each city, we determined the corresponding city for each company based on the location of registration specified in each registration record. Additionally, in the robustness tests, we considered the actual operating locations of the companies to accurately match them with their respective cities. We also referenced Feng and Li (2023), directly using the natural logarithm of the number of business entries in each prefecture-level city as an alternative measure of business entry ( _Entry2_ ). 

In measuring the core explanatory variable of this study, the fiscal pressure of prefecture-level city governments, we adopt the mainstream “fiscal revenue-expenditure gap method” (Bai et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2023), which characterizes fiscal pressure using the difference between general public budget expenditures and general public budget revenues. We then normalized the fiscal gap using general public budget revenues to derive the fiscal pressure index ( _FP1_ ), with reference to Liao and Liu (2014) and Zhu et al. (2020). For robustness, we also standardized it against the city’s GDP to calculate an alternative indicator ( _FP2_ ), as suggested by Sun and Zhang (2019) and Gong et al. (2022). ## **5. Further exploration** 

In this chapter, building upon the primary conclusions, we extend our research to examine the heterogeneous impacts of company characteristics and regional features on the main findings, as well as the spatial spillover effects of fiscal pressure among cities on the entry of new enterprises. 

## _5.1. Heterogeneity analysis_ 

Firstly, we categorized the enterprise entry indicator based on the ownership type, distinguishing between state-owned and non-stateowned enterprises. At the prefectural city level, we calculated the annual entry counts for both types of enterprises separately. This classification yields two distinct indicators: state-owned enterprise entry ( _Entry:Soe_ ) and non-state-owned enterprise entry ( _Entry:Non-Soe_ ). Regression analyses were subsequently conducted to examine the impact of fiscal pressure on these two indicators and determine whether the state-owned characteristic introduces heterogeneous effects on the primary conclusions. As the literature suggests that state-owned enterprises possess an advantage in obtaining credit (Cull et al., 2009; Hau, Yi, Shan, & Sheng, 2019; Liu et al., 2021), this advantage may significantly alleviate the credit resource crowding-out effect typically experienced by SMEs, especially when the SMEs are also state-owned. In this case, the phenomenon of “entry obstruction” attributed to fiscal pressure is unlikely to occur or may be somewhat mitigated. The results from Columns (1) and (2) of Table 10 indicate that fiscal pressure does not have a significant linear relationship with the entry of state-owned enterprises, yet it exhibits a suppressive effect on the entry of non-stateowned enterprises. This aligns with our conjecture. 

Secondly, we further elucidate the mechanistic role of governmental intervention in financial resources within its jurisdiction on the core findings of this study. To this end, we devised an indicator to quantify the degree of government intervention in the financial sector at the prefectural city level, which is then used to classify the sample cities. ## _5.2. Spatial spillover effects_ 

This study addresses the topic of new firm entry, a phenomenon that 

exhibits a pronounced spatial mobility pattern. This implies that focusing solely on the local perspective, while neglecting potential impacts from other regions, would impose significant limitations on the analysis. Furthermore, it is necessary to examine whether there exists a spatial correlation between two core variables: fiscal pressure and new firm entry. If such a correlation indeed exists, conclusions derived from traditional panel regression analysis could be biased. In addition, if local fiscal pressure inhibits the entry of new firms, does fiscal pressure in neighboring cities also produce spillover effects on local firm entry? Based on these considerations, this section will extend the analysis from a spatial spillover perspective. 

Prior to conducting spatial econometric analysis, it is necessary to first ascertain whether spatial correlation exists in both the explanatory and dependent variables of this study, and then select an appropriate spatial model. To achieve this, we initially conduct a Moran’s I test. As shown in Appendix **Table A.3** , the Moran’s I indices for both the fiscal pressure indicator, _FP1_ , and the enterprise entry indicator, _Entry1_ , are strongly significant in each year of the study period, rejecting the null hypothesis of “no spatial autocorrelation.” This highlights the need to apply a spatial econometric model to test the main conclusions of this study, with an initial suggestion to consider using a Spatial Durbin Model. Fig. 4 illustrates the spatial correlation of the core variables of this study, exemplified by the year 2020. 

The solid fitted line in the figure indicates that the fiscal stress indicator and the new firm entry indicator are both correlated with their respective spatial lag terms. Additionally, the Moran’s I statistic and its − “ corresponding p value reject the null hypothesis of no spatial correlation.” 

Furthermore, we employ the Lagrange Multiplier test to examine the necessity and appropriateness of incorporating spatial autoregressive terms and spatially correlated error terms into the spatial model. As indicated by the results in Table 11, the null hypotheses of “no spatial lag of the dependent variable” and “no spatial autocorrelation in the residuals” are both rejected. Therefore, further consideration of the GNSM may be necessary. To determine the suitability of this model, we utilize Likelihood Ratio (LR) tests to compare the applicability of various models, with the results presented in Table 12. The entry of new businesses serves as one of the driving forces and a manifestation of vitality in regional economic growth. Local governments are keen to adopt various preferential or welfare policies to foster such entrepreneurial activities. However, in the face of fiscal pressures, certain actions by the government may become distorted, thereby exerting a countervailing force against the entry of new enterprises. This paper, employing a two-way fixed effects model for analysis, finds that local government fiscal pressure significantly impedes the entry of new enterprises in the locality. This finding has been corroborated through a series of robustness checks. To mitigate potential endogeneity concerns in regression analysis, we additionally design a natural experiment based on the policy shock from the VAT revenue sharing reform to further validate the reliability of our findings. Mechanistic analysis indicates that local governments, by intervening in the allocation of local financial resources, channel these resources preferentially towards larger enterprises in the area, consequently crowding out credit availability for SMEs. This, in turn, poses obstacles to the establishment of new businesses. We also exclude the potential channels through which fiscal pressure might influence the entry of new enterprises by affecting government subsidies, tax collection intensity, or the business environment. 

Further analysis reveals that the state-owned nature of a business and

regional-level indicators such as the degree of government financial intervention and the level of inclusive financial development exert heterogeneous moderating effects on the main conclusion. Moreover, through spatial econometric analysis, we observe a spatial spillover effect of fiscal pressure on new enterprise entry. Specifically, when neighboring cities experience greater fiscal pressure, it tends to “propel” new enterprises to enter the focal city. Overall, this paper reveals a seemingly paradoxical phenomenon: while local governments aspire to encourage the entry of more new enterprises, their fiscal pressures lead them to mobilize local financial resources in a way that diminishes the financial accessibility for SMEs, thereby inadvertently impeding the establishment of new businesses. This reflects the unintended negative impact on local entrepreneurship by the government in its attempts to address fiscal difficulties. Our research endeavors to enrich the scholarly discourse on the determinants of enterprise entry and aims to offer a novel perspective in the study of the impact of local government fiscal pressure. Furthermore, this study contributes empirical evidence supporting the theory of the government’s “interventionist hand” and delves into analyzing its corresponding consequences. These findings offer valuable insights for the government to promote local entrepreneurship more effectively and sustainably. Admittedly, this study has certain limitations, primarily the lack of an economic mathematical model to support the arguments. Moreover, the empirical analysis faced considerable constraints due to data availability. These limitations 

underscore areas that require further improvement in future research, particularly in exploring the micro-level impacts of fiscal pressure or the effects of government financial intervention.

- _Local fiscal pressure:_ the financial strain experienced by local governments, specifically the imbalance between expenditures and revenues within a certain period due to liquidity challenges. 

- _New Firm Entry:_ the registration and commencement of operations by newly established firms in a particular region. It is essentially reflected in the growth of newly registered firms, serving as an indicator of entrepreneurial vitality and regional dynamism. 

- _Governmental intervention:_ the deliberate interference by the government in the allocation and management of economic resources within its jurisdiction. 

- _The reform of replacing business tax with value-added tax (B2V reform):_ formation in China’s fiscal and taxation system. This reform aims primarily to reduce a significant transthe double taxation imposed on enterprises by substituting the business tax with a value-added tax. 

- _Tax enforcement:_ defined in relevant literature and referenced in this paper, refers to the intensity with which the government executes taxation on taxable entities within its jurisdiction. 

- _Local government financing vehicles:_ companies established by local governments in China with the primary purpose of raising funds. Prior to the 2014 revision of the Budget Law, most local governments in China were not legally authorized to borrow directly.
