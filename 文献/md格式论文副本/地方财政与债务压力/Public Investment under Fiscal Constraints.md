## **Abstract** 

The new member states (NMS) of the European Union must comply with the Stability and Growth Pact (SGP) and the investment goals implied by the Lisbon Agenda. However, the SGP rules may result in underinvestment or distortions in the allocation of public expenditure. This paper provides new evidence on the effects of debt sustainability and the SGP fiscal constraints on government expenditure on fixed capital, education and health in OECD countries by estimating government expenditure reaction functions to public debt and cyclical conditions. We find that, at high levels of debt, government capital expenditure and education expenditure are significantly reduced as the debt ratio increases in all OECD countries independently of EMU (or EU) membership. ## **I. Introduction** 

The new member states of the European Union must comply with the Stability and Growth Pact (SGP) and the investment goals implied by the Lisbon Agenda. A trade-off may thus emerge between fiscal targets and economic growth: fiscal constraints may lead to underinvestment or distortions in the allocation of public expenditure and, thus, to lower growth. The aim of this paper is to investigate whether fiscal adjustment needed to ensure debt sustainability or the requirements of the SGP may affect public investment in fixed capital and expenditure on education and health. To assess the impact of the SGP on public investment, the experience of EMU member states is compared with that of other OECD countries. 

The constraints implied by debt sustainability or fiscal rules, together with the government’s inability to cut consumption or raise taxes, can lead to underinvestment, suboptimal policies and distortions in the allocation of public resources. Fiscal adjustment may hit investment projects that are economically efficient but lack political support and are easier to cut than other expenditures because of weaker opposition from citizens. The SGP, in particular, by imposing limits to the overall budget deficit rather than focusing on debt sustainability,[1] may: (a) lead to lower investment (Balassone and Franco, 2000); (b) favour projects with higher short-term returns over projects with higher returns in the long run (Blanchard and Giavazzi, 2004; Easterly, Irwin and Servén, 2008); and (c) lead to asset decumulation or implicit liabilities (Easterly, 1999) – for example, to inefficient privatisation worsening the fiscal position in the long run (Buiter, 1997; Florio, 2004 and 2007). Advocates of the ‘golden rule of public finances’, that governments can borrow to fund investment, contend that a limit on the overall government deficit may lead to underinvestment because

Balassone and Franco (2000) present descriptive evidence of a potential negative effect of SGP rules on public investment in fixed capital. In particular, focusing on lasting and significant budget consolidation episodes in EU-15 member states between 1980 and 1997, they find that in 25 out of 32 cases, fiscal adjustment was obtained through investment cuts. With some exceptions, the larger reductions occurred in high-debt countries, whereas low-debt countries could increase deficits and investment during cyclical downturns in the 1990s. This opportunity was not available to high-debt countries where fiscal consolidation was still under way and, as a result, the investment to GDP ratio was significantly lower than in low-debt countries. 

On the other hand, Galí and Perotti (2003), focusing on EMU countries versus other OECD economies, find that the fall in public investment relative to GDP over the last two decades was no greater in the EU than in other countries. They conclude that the reduction of public investment has been the result of a secular decline affecting all countries at the same time more than the effect of the Maastricht Treaty and the SGP. 

We complement and extend previous research in many ways. First, as in Galí and Perotti (2003), we compare public investment in EMU countries with that in other OECD economies, but we explicitly account for debt sustainability motivations by distinguishing between high- and low-debt countries. Indeed, to the extent that debt sustainability constraints are likely to bind only at high levels of debt, their impact should be non-linear, as implicitly recognised by Balassone and Franco (2000). Moreover, we extend the sample of countries to include new member states (NMS) in order to examine the effect of EU membership on their investment decisions. Looking at the NMS separately is important not only because they are a main focus of the analysis, but because they are quite different from the EU15 countries: they are catching-up economies, with lower public capital stocks and generally lower debt to GDP ratios and higher deficit ratios (see, for example, Orbàn and Szapàry (2004)).[2] 

Finally, unlike previous studies, we do not restrict our attention to government capital formation but we also examine government expenditure on education and health. In fact, physical capital accumulation is just one of the many dimensions of public investment; expenditures on education, health, research and technology are surely as important as infrastructure

Likewise, little attention has been paid to the effects of fiscal consolidation on the composition of government expenditure, even though fiscal adjustment may affect growth by changing not only the composition between current and capital expenditure but also the allocation of public funds to different types of expenditures.[3] Looking at government expenditure on education and health is a first attempt toward a better understanding of the composition effects of fiscal restraint, which is crucial for a better design of fiscal rules as well as for a better allocation of EU resources among alternative projects. 

We provide new evidence on the effects of debt sustainability and SGP fiscal constraints on government expenditure on fixed capital, education and health in OECD countries by estimating government expenditure reaction functions to public debt and cyclical conditions. In particular, we estimate panel regressions with country fixed effects where we distinguish between countries with low and high debt to GDP ratios and between EMU (or EU) member states and other OECD countries to examine whether public investment decisions differ across country groups depending on their fiscal position and membership. Our research strategy is based on the idea that a reduction of public investment (relative to GDP or total government expenditure) should only be observed (or be stronger) at high levels of debt, when debt sustainability is at risk, unless budget deficits are bound by fiscal rules, such as the SGP.[4] Indeed, absent such rules, only highly indebted countries experiencing debt sustainability problems should reduce public investment as the debt ratio increases. At high levels of debt, we find evidence of a strong negative reaction of both government capital expenditure and education expenditure to the debt ratio. These effects are significant in all highly indebted OECD countries independently of EMU (or EU) membership. By contrast, neither capital expenditure nor education expenditure is affected by the debt ratio in lowdebt countries. These findings are robust to the inclusion of the government deficit in the panel regressions. 

Hence, it appears that EU countries have been constrained in their investment decisions more by the need to ensure debt sustainability than by the rules of the SGP. At high levels of debt, debt sustainability imposes a constraint on fiscal policy that is strong enough to distort the allocation of government expenditure even in the absence of formal fiscal rules. In particular, public investments in fixed capital and education are penalised relative to other types of expenditures as the debt increases. In light of the European debt crisis, these findings paint a gloomy picture for the growth prospects of highly indebted EU economies. 

Finally, in low-debt NMS countries, fixed capital formation also appears not to be affected by the SGP rules. In these countries, public investment even increases with the debt ratio, a finding that is reassuring for their growth prospects. A different picture, however, emerges when we focus on expenditures on public health and education, as it appears that NMS governments cut such expenditures – even at low levels of debt – as the deficit increases. Problems in controlling total expenditure, high deficits and the rules of the preventive arm of the SGP may have penalised investment in human capital in NMS countries while leaving fixed capital investment unaffected.

The estimation results for government capital formation are presented and discussed in Section III. Section IV does the same for government expenditures on education and health. ## **IV. Public expenditures on education and health in OECD countries** 

Public investment in fixed capital does not exhaust the possible channels through which the government can stimulate economic growth. Modern growth theory emphasises the importance of human capital for growth: knowledge is considered a public good generating positive externalities on productivity. 

Lucas (1988) argues that investment in education increases GDP due to human capital externalities. The latter arise if the presence of educated workers makes other workers more productive, creating learning opportunities through social and professional interactions. Another channel is the facilitation of adoption and creation of new ideas and technologies. Finally, externalities can be generated by a reduction of crime, improved health and higher tax revenues (McMahon, 1999). 

The benefits of human capital are the main rationale for government involvement in education (Hanushek, 2002). As human capital generates

positive externalities, mainly due to the link with technological change and to the indirect positive effect of education on productivity, the social rate of return is larger than the private one. This implies that the evaluation of expenditure on education programmes should take into account these spillovers in order to obtain reliable estimates of the effects of government policies. 

Despite the importance of the contribution of human capital, uncertainties remain about the magnitude of its social return because of various econometric and measurement problems (Benhabib and Spiegel, 1994; Bassanini and Scarpetta, 2001). Taking into account positive externalities, some studies show that the return on investment in human capital can be higher than that on investment in physical capital. For EU countries, de la Fuente and Ciccone (2003) report an estimated direct return on schooling expenditure comparable to that of physical capital; they claim that, once non-market returns to education and benefits for social cohesion are considered, the returns may be even higher. For Spain, de la Fuente, Domenech and Jimeno (2003) show that the social return on human capital is higher than the return on investment in physical capital. 

Another type of government expenditure that contributes to human capital formation is health expenditure. While such expenditure is often neglected, empirical evidence shows that it has a positive and significant effect on aggregate output (Rivera and Currais, 1999; Bloom, Canning and Sevilla, 2004). Moreover, the interactions between the different types of expenditure, such as education and health, reinforce mutual spillovers and growth (Agenor and Neanidis, 2006).

More importantly, we find evidence that health expenditure is reduced as the deficit increases, both in highly indebted EU-15 countries and in low-debt NMS countries where the effect is significant no matter how health expenditure is normalised. Indeed, as for education expenditure, it appears that NMS governments have cut expenditure on public health as total expenditure and the deficit increased, even at low levels of debt. 

The finding of a similar negative relation between health expenditure and the government deficit at high levels of debt in both EMU member states and other OECD countries and the absence of such a relation in low-debt EMU and EU-15 members may suggest that the formal constraints of the SGP have not made a substantial difference. However, there is some evidence that problems in controlling total expenditure, high deficits and the rules of the preventive arm of the SGP may have distorted expenditure allocation in NMS countries. 

## **V. Conclusions** 

We have documented that, at high levels of debt, governments cut capital expenditure, as the debt ratio increases, in all OECD countries independently of EMU (or EU) membership. Government expenditure on education is also reduced relative to other expenditures in highly indebted OECD countries as the debt ratio increases. The effect is as strong outside the euro area as in EMU countries. By contrast, neither capital expenditure nor education expenditure is affected by the debt ratio in low-debt countries. 

The SGP does not appear to set an additional constraint on the fiscal policies of EMU (or EU-15) member states in that capital expenditure and education expenditure are significantly reduced in all highly indebted OECD countries. Furthermore, the negative reaction of capital and education expenditures to the debt ratio is robust to the inclusion of the government deficit in the panel regressions. 

Our findings are consistent with the evidence on capital expenditure provided by Galí and Perotti (2003), who argue against the relevance of the constraints imposed by the SGP, but qualify their results in an important way: the decline of public investment observed in all OECD countries is due not just to historical factors but to a common concern for debt sustainability.

The same concern negatively affects education expenditure, whereas health expenditure appears to be unrelated to the debt ratio. 

At high levels of debt, debt sustainability imposes a constraint on fiscal policy that is strong enough to distort the allocation of government expenditure even in the absence of formal rules such as the SGP. We have shown that this behaviour can be easily rationalised in a simple growth model where the government must cut public investment in order to ensure debt sustainability as the gross debt reaches a threshold level. 

Fixed capital formation in low-debt new member states also appears not to be affected by the rules of the SGP. In these countries, public investment even increases with the debt ratio, a finding that is reassuring for their growth prospects. A different picture, however, emerges when we focus on expenditures on public health and education, as it appears that NMS governments cut such expenditures – even at low levels of debt – as the deficit increases. Problems in controlling total expenditure, high deficits and the rules of the preventive arm of the SGP may have penalised investment in human capital while leaving fixed capital investment unaffected. 

Our analysis points to important distortionary effects of fiscal restraint on the allocation of public funds that penalise fixed capital and human capital formation at high levels of debt. Indeed, debt sustainability concerns (or high deficits in the case of the NMS) may lead to significant composition effects on public expenditure. Such effects appear substantial in the case of the NMS, where the shares of education and health expenditure fall with an increase in government deficit, while capital expenditure does not suffer from high deficits. A tentative explanation of this evidence is that investment in fixed capital is favoured over investment in human capital, say in education, because of its greater reversibility. Alternatively, human capital income could be perceived as a less reliable source of revenues when migration opportunities make labour income a much more elastic tax base than capital income. 

While the effects of fiscal consolidation on government capital expenditure have been studied widely, there has so far been scant attention given to the allocation of public resources between investment in fixed capital and in human capital and across different types of investment. Though preliminary, the evidence that we provide suggests that composition effects are substantial and that understanding their determinants is an important task for future research.
